## The question before the council

Does the implemented Marketing Gauntlet now describe the whole Filosage product honestly, serve its broad eligible learner audience without reducing it to one professional cohort or to English learners, and avoid advertising capabilities the app does not actually offer? A false positive would put unsupported promises into the product; a false negative would hide useful capabilities from learners who are already eligible to use them.

- public landing, library, pricing, FAQ, evidence example, terms, support, and sitemap surfaces;
- signed-in Course Studio, course discovery/start, learner evidence, home return recommendation, and schedule language;

The final claim scan found no professional-only, English-learner-only, credential, guaranteed-mastery, personalized-recommendation, or generally-available flag-gated claim in the application. ## Seated: April Dunford, Seth Godin, Claude Hopkins, Byron Sharp (council session)

Dunford tests the category and alternative set; Godin tests audience fit and trust; Hopkins tests claims and response measurement; Sharp is the designated dissenter on narrow targeting and differentiation.

---

### April Dunford — category and competitive context

Dunford's sequence starts with the alternative: learners currently stitch together generic chat, videos, articles, marketplace courses, notes, and standalone review tools. The implemented category—“self-directed learning and course-creation workspace”—makes the connected goal, course, practice, review, source state, and evidence record understandable without inventing a new market. The value now follows from capabilities the repository can prove.

The important correction is that “product/data professional” is a campaign cohort, not the product audience. The canonical context now distinguishes product eligibility from initial validation cohorts, while the four editable library starts make study, personal project, career, and work situations visible. The English-interface/non-English-course distinction also survives into the public FAQ, Course Studio, telemetry, and acceptance tests.

**Bottom line:** Keep the broad product category and run bounded campaigns by learning situation; never let a campaign segment overwrite the capability contract.

### Seth Godin — fit, trust, and the change offered

Godin's first questions are now answerable: it is for eligible independent learners aged 13 or older and individual creators; it helps them turn a meaningful goal into practice and evidence they can inspect. The change is not “become a professional” or “learn English.” It is moving from disconnected resources and passive consumption to one learner-controlled capability cycle.

The strongest trust device is restraint. The public evidence example announces that it is fictional, distinguishes reported, observed, assessed, and unresolved states, and disclaims credentials. Job-start buttons admit they start editable searches rather than personalized recommendations. Closed billing remains visibly closed. That generosity is more distinctive than another “learn anything with AI” claim.

**Bottom line:** The promise is appropriately broad in eligibility and specific in the change it offers; earn permission by preserving the stated limits as the product expands.

### Claude Hopkins — reason-why claims and measurable response

Hopkins would approve of replacing adjectives with inspectable reasons: a visitor can open a complete public outline, see the source state, inspect a fixed evidence example, and understand what account access changes. The flagship course is a product sample, not decorative testimony. Plus and Pro prompts name the exact additional action and preserve the learner's current task.

He would not call the marketing successful yet. The new metrics correctly keep anonymous acquisition separate from verified activation, anchor retention after first practice, exclude owner traffic, and treat a pricing query as context rather than purchase intent. Those are sound test instruments. They are not results. The four learning situations remain market-unvalidated until qualified interviews and behavior pass the documented gate.

**Bottom line:** The implementation passes the claim-and-measurement gate, but only real participant response can pass the demand gate.

### Byron Sharp — reach, entry points, and distinctiveness

Sharp's dissent is that a smallest-audience strategy can become an accidental ceiling. The user's objection was correct: students, self-taught people, career changers, and professionals are category buyers entering through different situations. The four job starts create useful category entry points without promising a recommendation system. The product should be available to all eligible buyers even while acquisition experiments focus on one cohort.

He would also distinguish product differentiation from memory. “Capability Cycle,” the calm editorial system, the Filosage mark, source-state language, and “evidence you can inspect” are promising assets, but repository coherence does not prove unaided recognition. Reusing them consistently matters more than inventing fresh positioning for each campaign.

**Bottom line:** Preserve broad reach and repeat the same distinctive learning-and-evidence assets across every campaign; do not mistake a targeted test for the size of the market.

## Where the council disagrees

1. **Smallest viable audience versus broad category reach** — Godin wants one highly relevant cohort; Sharp warns that tight targeting caps discovery. The trade-off is campaign precision versus product exclusion. The implemented resolution is correct: broad eligibility, four visible entry points, and cohort-keyed experiments. Cohort activation and acquisition efficiency—not opinion—should determine campaign allocation.
2. **Differentiation versus distinctiveness** — Dunford emphasizes the connected Capability Cycle and evidence-state separation; Sharp asks whether anyone remembers those attributes as Filosage. The trade-off is persuasive explanation versus mental availability. Message comprehension, unaided recall, and keyed course discovery by entry point would settle it.
3. **A truthful sample versus a proven market** — Godin and Dunford see a coherent, trustworthy promise; Hopkins refuses to infer demand from repository quality. The trade-off is readiness to test versus evidence of traction. The hypothesis register's interview, course-discovery, first-practice, and meaningful-return gates must settle it.

## Chair's synthesis

**Verdict: pass the capability, claims, implementation, and local acceptance gate. Hold the demand-validation, paid-launch, push, deployment, and production-health gates.**

No unsupported feature was added to public plan copy. Runtime-gated flashcards and V2 surfaces remain absent from general marketing; paid checkout remains locked; course-language support is not confused with interface localization; portable evidence is not described as a credential; and local tests are not presented as customer or production proof.

- **Do:** Use the implemented four entry points to run one bounded campaign at a time; record qualified interviews and the separated acquisition, activation, and return metrics; keep the canonical capability register authoritative when future copy changes.
- **Do:** Reuse the Capability Cycle, source-state language, and inspectable-evidence framing consistently so audience breadth does not turn into generic messaging.
- **Tripwire:** Hopkins's warning governs the next decision. If interviews, qualified course discovery, first practice, and return behavior do not advance, revise the campaign hypothesis—not the product eligibility boundary and not the evidence standard.
- **Tripwire:** Sharp's warning governs segmentation. If one cohort performs better, treat that as a channel/campaign result unless repeated evidence supports a genuine product-market boundary.
- **Execute with:** customer research and A/B testing only after a separate approved launch/traffic plan; analytics already has the required event and identity boundaries.
